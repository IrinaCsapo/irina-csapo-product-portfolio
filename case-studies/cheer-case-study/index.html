Case study · Lovable SheBuilds · October 2026

Cheer

A support network, not a social network.

Role
Solo. Research, product, UX, UI, brand, copy, accessibility, QA and release.
Built in
Several days, submitted 18 October 2026.
Context
Lovable’s SheBuilds, a global hackathon with around 800 women taking part.
Built with
Lovable, TanStack Start and Supabase. Claude for planning, debugging and release.

01 Where it started

Fifteen applications, and nobody knew

A friend once told me she’d sent fifteen job applications that week and hadn’t mentioned it to anyone. Not because nothing happened, but because nothing had worked yet.

It turned out she wasn’t alone. When I surveyed 25 people, most of those working towards something hard said they keep their small wins to themselves, or tell just one person. Yet so much of their effort was invisible.

“15 applications a week.” “Gym every morning, meal prep every Sunday.” What people were actually doing, and not telling anyone about
  • 25people surveyed
  • 15 of 23keep small wins to themselves, or tell one person
  • 3ever post about it publicly

02 The problem

The reasons were social, not practical

Nobody was short of a place to post. They were short of a reason to feel alright about it. Sharing felt like bragging, said 10 people. Another 10 said they’d feel exposed if it didn’t work out. Nine didn’t want to bother anyone.

Supporters struggled too, which I hadn’t expected. 10 of 17 weren’t always sure when to check in or what to say. The wish to help was there; the timing and the words were not.

And being noticed mattered more than the numbers suggested it would.

A friend’s acknowledgement “made me feel proud of myself, which I didn’t before”. Survey respondent

The doubters were just as useful. They asked why this wasn’t simply a WhatsApp group, and worried about pressure, judgement and privacy. Those three objections became the brief.

03 The solution

Three blockers, removed

Cheer is designed around what makes a group chat feel wrong for this. Each of the three objections has a specific answer in the product.

  • Posting became logging

    A private record first, with a “just for me” option, so nothing has to feel like an announcement.

  • Effort, not outcomes

    The unit is what you did, not what came of it. “12 applications” cannot fail.

  • No account for supporters

    Squad members join through a simple link. Nothing to download, nothing to sign up for.

Supporters can cheer with a tap, send a note, send a card that opens like a little gift, or send an “I’m here” on hard days. Every cheer carries a line that solves the supporter’s problem as much as the sender’s.

The best support is “a message that doesn’t need a reply”. A friend, who gave me the line that is now on every cheer

Gifting stayed a light, milestone-only idea rather than a feature I built out. The research was clear that words are the core of support, and I would rather ship the thing people asked for than the thing that is easier to demo.

04 The results

Small numbers, early, and real

  • 16 of 25reacted “Love it” or “Interesting”
  • 63visits in the first days, from 8 countries
  • 4.5 minaverage per visit
  • 23 · 11people sent cheers · people sent cards

These are small, early figures and I would rather report them honestly than dress them up. The more useful result was what real friends did with it.

They found issues no test caught. A card that never arrived, because a supporter flow could report success without having saved. Fixing the things actual people hit shaped the product more than any new feature would have.

05 What’s next

A weekly round-up, where people already are

Cheer is a mobile-first prototype, not yet the full app. The research points clearly at what comes next: a weekly round-up, so supporters get the timing and the context they told me they were missing.

It should arrive through WhatsApp or email, so nobody needs another app to be a good friend. Native iOS and Android come after that, with gentle nudges rather than constant notifications.
