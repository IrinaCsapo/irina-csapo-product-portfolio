01 Code as a design tool

"You'll use code as a design tool, not simply as an implementation medium." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Vera, built in two days because the question needed answering

Vera checks supplement and wellness claims against scientific evidence. It takes three kinds of input: paste a social link, upload a screenshot, or search a brand name. I led product design and engineering, at the ZOE Women in Tech Hackathon in London in July 2026, in a team of six who were mostly strangers.

The interesting part is not the build, it is what the build settled. You cannot tell from a design file whether a trust score feels credible when the data behind it is thin. So the prototype pulls real numbers: a Cloudflare Worker fetches posts server‑side, a model with search grounding identifies the product, and OpenAlex returns live counts of peer‑reviewed studies. Once real data was flowing, the design question changed from "how should this look" to "what do we do when we cannot verify something", which is a much better question to be arguing about on day one.

  • 2 daysidea to working app
  • 3 inputslink, screenshot, brand
  • Live dataOpenAlex study counts

Why I build rather than mock: a prototype in code exists to answer a question you cannot answer in a design file. Everything else is decoration. Open Vera or read the case study.

02 Prototyping before implementation

"Rapidly prototype workflows, interactions and usability concepts before implementation." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Omaze Monthly Millionaire, where the data ended the layout argument

Omaze had raised its subscription price from £10 to £15. Monthly Millionaire, a £1m monthly draw, launched as a free add‑on to subscriptions and also pay as you go. It opened at roughly £650k a month against a forecast above £1m.

Two pieces of data decided the direction before any visual work started. Meta funnel analysis showed mobile users interacting predominantly with the bottom half of the screen, and internal data showed a customer base skewing 45 and over. That turned an opinion argument about layout into a rebuild with a clear value hierarchy above the fold, persistent thumb‑reachable calls to action, winner testimonials, and larger, more legible type.

Then three experiments, one at a time, so each result meant something.

  • +7.3%TrustPilot rating and testimonials above the fold
  • +4.8%winner reaction video as a looping hero
  • +8.5%subscription surfaced early as "Best Value", anchored on entries rather than price

The decision I am proudest of is the third one, because it carried real cannibalisation risk. Pushing subscription earlier could have eaten pay as you go, so we measured both sides rather than just the number that would have looked good. It did not cannibalise. Overall the work delivered a 35% conversion uplift, £1.5m in a month, sustained above £1m. Separately, checkout bolt‑ons lifted attachment rate by 27%. Read the case study.

03 Complex enterprise software

"Design UX for complex enterprise SaaS applications and supporting customer experiences." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Veva, one system for two users with nothing in common

Veva is a mobile app controlling EV home charge points. It has two audiences who share almost no context: the homeowner who lives with it for years, and the installing engineer who meets it once, on a driveway, under time pressure. There is a dedicated engineer mode because those are genuinely different products wearing one badge.

I designed the installer's flow first. The install is the gate: if connecting the unit fails on the driveway, the homeowner never reaches the product at all, and the support cost lands immediately. So the app uses smart detection to find the charger, with a local hotspot fallback for when the home network is not usable at install time, rather than assuming a working home network is available at the one moment it usually is not.

For the homeowner side, charging status runs through eight animated states built in Rive and exported as JSON, using a pulsating dial as a metaphor for current. Alongside that: multi‑user and multi‑car support, schedules, spend analysis over time, cost tracking and remote management.

No metrics exist for this project, so I am not going to give you any. The team was one product designer, which was me, plus a business analyst, a product manager and an iOS and an Android engineer.

My second example is Paycada, an automated invoice chasing platform in regulated fintech, where I was sole designer for three years from zero to one, across the platform, the website and the brand. Veva case study · Paycada case study.

04 Accessibility and WCAG

"Solid understanding of accessibility standards and WCAG." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

The audit I ran, and the failure in my own portfolio

On Paycada I ran a WCAG audit of the live product and it found two failures that were already shipped. That is the useful kind of audit, the one that costs you something.

Here is a more uncomfortable example. The hero on my own portfolio sets the word "experiences" in an animated gradient that runs from orange through coral to pink. Measured from the live CSS, those stops sit at 2.1:1, 3.1:1 and 3.7:1 against the near‑white hero. Large display text needs 3:1, so for part of every cycle that word fails, and it has been failing in public for a while.

On this page the same orange is darkened to 3.4:1, so every stop of the equivalent gradient clears 3:1. Every other colour here was checked at the size it is used, and body text runs at 8.3:1. I would rather show you the working than assert the knowledge, so the tool below is the real WCAG calculation: sRGB linearisation, the 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 coefficients, and the 0.05 offsets. It opens on my own failure.

05 Design systems and coded components

"Contribute to the design system, reusable UI patterns and coded component libraries." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Three tiers, and the drift problem I have not solved yet

Paycada's system ended up in three tiers: brand primitives, semantic aliases, then component tokens, with full state coverage on components. I want to be accurate about how that happened. It emerged in the work rather than being architected on day one. I articulated it as three tiers afterwards, once the shape was obvious, and that retrospective clarity is what made it teachable.

The part I find genuinely unsolved is what happens to a system when you build with AI assistance. The failure mode is not broken code. Broken code announces itself. The failure mode is plausible code: a component that is ninety per cent right, uses a hard‑coded hex instead of the semantic token, and passes review because it looks correct. Ninety per cent right is more dangerous than fifty per cent wrong, because nobody goes looking.

My current patch is to write the system's rules into a file the agent reads before it writes anything, so the constraints arrive before the code does. It helps. It is not a solution, and drift is still detectable by eye rather than by tooling.

This is work I would like to do properly with a team that has a real system to protect. If AI tooling is going to accelerate how much interface gets produced, then the interesting problem is not generating components faster, it is keeping a system coherent while it happens.

06 AI-assisted experiences

"Design and prototype AI-assisted experiences: conversational interfaces, guided configuration, intelligent recommendations." Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

The decision that made Vera trustworthy

The hardest call in Vera was what to do when the system cannot verify something.

If it cannot verify a field, that field comes back blank rather than invented.

A blank field looks worse in a demo. It is the only honest option in a product about evidence, because a plausible guess in a trust tool is not a small bug, it is the whole product failing quietly. Two consequences followed from it.

First, Vera scores the claim against the evidence, never the brand or the person making it. That keeps it a product about substance rather than a popularity engine, and it is why likes are deliberately excluded: they read as an endorsement the product has not earned. Second, the Claim Score is labelled openly as a prototype heuristic, and study counts link out to their sources, so a sceptical user can go and check.

The principle I would bring to identity work: users do not need a system to always be right, they need to be able to tell when it is not. That matters more, not less, when the automation is deciding whether someone is who they say they are, and the cost of a confident wrong answer is somebody either locked out or let in.

07 Straight answers

What I don't have

Identity, authentication and cybersecurity

None. I have not worked in the domain and I am not going to pretend the vocabulary is familiar. What I do have is the instinct the domain runs on: every security product lives on the tension between verification and friction, and the design job is deciding, step by step, which side you pay on. Regulated fintech and healthtech means designing inside constraints you cannot argue with is normal for me, so I expect to be slow on your terminology and quick on your tradeoffs.

Production engineering inside someone else's codebase

I ship my own products end to end, but I have not worked inside a large React codebase owned by a team of engineers. I direct and review rather than write from a blank file. I understand what I ship, I can debug it without the assistant in the room, and I can explain every decision in it. I would not claim to own your production architecture, and I would not want a role that needed me to.

Primary qualitative research

My research is behavioural and quantitative, plus usability testing: funnel data, experiments, watching people use the thing. I have not led an end to end interview programme with recruitment, discussion guide and synthesis. It is the gap I am actively closing, and on Strands I ran the research myself for emotionally sensitive health design, which is the closest I have come.

08 First weeks

What I'd do first

  • Learn the domain by watching real admins work. Not a doc crawl. The vocabulary sticks when you have seen someone hesitate over a setting and then explain why.
  • Find where people stall and prototype one fix. One coded prototype of the worst moment in a real workflow is worth more than a survey of all of them.
  • Audit the design system for drift. Where has the shipped interface quietly stopped matching the tokens, and is that drift a mistake or a pattern the system has not caught up with yet.
  • Keep asking which decision would tell us we were wrong. It is the question that turned the Omaze work from opinion into evidence, and it is the one I would bring to every review.
