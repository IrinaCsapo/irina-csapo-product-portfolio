02 Code as a design tool

“Use code as a design tool, not simply as an implementation medium.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Vera is my clearest example. A mobile prototype that checks supplement and wellness claims from TikTok and Instagram against scientific evidence: paste a link, upload a screenshot, or search a brand. I led product design and engineering, at the ZOE Women in Tech Hackathon in London in July 2026, in a team of six.

Its claim score is a heuristic, but the numbers behind it are real. A Cloudflare Worker fetches posts server-side, a model with search grounding identifies the product, and OpenAlex returns live counts of peer-reviewed studies.

03 Prototyping before implementation

“Rapidly prototype workflows and usability concepts before implementation.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Prototyping to explore is one thing. Prototyping to settle an argument is where it gets interesting.

Omaze UK's Monthly Millionaire launched at around £650k a month against a £1m+ forecast. Funnel data showed mobile users engaging almost entirely with the bottom half of the screen, on a customer base skewing 40 and over. 89% of them are on mobile. So we stopped debating layout and rebuilt around reachable actions, value and trust above the fold, and legible type.

Then one hypothesis at a time.

  • +7.3%trust signals above the fold
  • +4.8%winner reaction video hero
  • +8.5%subscription reframed as best value

The third carried real risk: the page led with pay as you go, so promoting the subscription could just have moved revenue between pockets. We measured both. It didn't. 35% conversion uplift, £1.5m in a month, sustained above £1m.

04 Complex enterprise software

“Design user experiences for complex enterprise SaaS applications.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Paycada is the one I'd bring to this. An automated invoice-chasing platform in regulated fintech, where I was sole designer for three years: platform, marketing site, brand and the design system underneath.

Chasing an invoice means asking a customer for money without damaging the relationship. So the work was about control and tone: what goes out automatically, to whom, what a human sees first, and how far an escalation travels before someone decides.

Veva is a different complexity: one system, two users with nothing in common. The homeowner who lives with an EV charge point for years, and the engineer with one visit to make it work. I designed for both, with the edge cases, error states and separate journeys each needs, to cut friction out of installation and catch hardware faults fast.

07 Accessibility and WCAG

“Solid understanding of accessibility standards and WCAG guidelines.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

On Paycada I ran a contrast audit and found two live failures that had shipped, because nobody had checked a hover state against its own background.

I check rather than assume, including my own work. Measured from live CSS, the word "experiences" in my portfolio hero runs as a gradient with stops at 2.1:1, 3.0:1 and 3.7:1. Large display text needs 3:1, so for part of every cycle it fails. Every accent here is your red at 5.4:1, which clears both thresholds.

08 Design systems and coded components

“Contribute to the design system, reusable patterns and coded component libraries.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Paycada's system emerged in the work rather than being formalised upfront. I articulated it afterwards as three tiers: brand primitives, semantic aliases, then component tokens, with full state coverage, so nobody invents a disabled style at four on a Friday.

What I care about now is drift. Building with AI assistance, the failure mode isn't broken code, it's plausible code: an invented spacing value, a near-miss colour, a component rebuilt instead of reused. 90% right is more dangerous than 50%, because nobody goes looking.

My patch is writing the system's rules into a file the agent reads first. It helps, but it's a patch on something structural, and making it into something a team can rely on is work I'd like to do properly.

09 AI-native tooling

“Leverage AI-native tools to accelerate ideation and generate realistic prototype data.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Realistic data is the whole game

Placeholder text tests nothing. People respond to content, not layout, and lorem ipsum hides the problems you needed to find: the name that's too long, the empty state nobody designed, the number that breaks the column. Vera ran on live data for exactly that reason.

Three versions beat one argument

Building fast means putting three real directions in front of a team instead of defending one. The conversation changes from "do you like it" to "which of these is wrong, and why".

Speed is the smaller half of it

The gain isn't hours saved, it's how many questions you're willing to ask. When a try costs twenty minutes rather than two days, you test the idea you'd have talked yourself out of.

10 AI-assisted experiences

“Design and prototype AI-assisted experiences.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

The part of the role I want most, and the decision it forced.

Vera's users are sold to by influencers they already trust, and we were asking them to move some of that trust to us. A model will always produce something. Half-recognise a product, invent plausible ingredients, and we'd be doing what the influencer does, in a more authoritative voice.

If the system can't verify a field, that field comes back blank rather than invented.

It looks less capable on some inputs. That was the trade, because one fabricated ingredient list destroys the premise. So we score the claim against the evidence rather than the brand, link study counts to their source, and label the score a prototype heuristic rather than science.

Users don't need a system to always be right. They need to be able to tell when it isn't.

What I'd want to prototype here

Guided configuration, of your three. An admin setting identity policy is making a decision with consequences, so a proposed setting has to show where it came from, how confident it is, and what changes if it's wrong. Model proposes, human commits, evidence on the value itself, correction in one interaction.

A pattern rather than a screen, which is why it belongs in a component library.

11 Why you

Why Ping

Two reasons, both things I think I can help solve rather than admire from a distance.

You've said the future isn't settled yet. Design and implementation are converging, and you want someone to help define what that looks like here. I've been inside that convergence for two years and already hit the failure modes, drift being the big one. I'd arrive with working opinions rather than theories, and I'd rather develop them with a team than alone at my kitchen table.

And trust is becoming a design problem for you. Your products decide whether someone is who they say they are. I've had to answer what a system should do when it isn't sure: a confident wrong answer gets accepted, a visible gap gets checked.

12 Straight answers

What I don't have

Identity, authentication and cybersecurity

None, and I'd be learning it. What I bring is regulated fintech and healthtech, so designing inside constraints you cannot argue with is familiar. Every security product lives on the same tension: more verification means more friction, and fewer people finish. You only choose, step by step, which side you pay. I'd want to find out where Ping pays it.

Production engineering inside someone else's codebase

I build and ship my own products, with Claude Code daily, but I direct and review rather than write from a blank file, and I haven't worked inside a large team codebase. I read what I ship, I can debug it, and I can explain every decision in it. I wouldn't claim to own your production architecture.

Primary qualitative research

Mine is behavioural and quantitative, plus usability testing. I haven't led an end-to-end interview programme. It's the thing I'm most actively closing.

13 What happens next

Where this goes

In the first month

  • Learn the domain by watching real admins configure something, not by reading the docs.
  • Find where people stall in your flows, and prototype one fix rather than argue about three.
  • Audit the design system for drift: the gap between what's documented and what's in production.
  • Keep asking which decision would tell us we were wrong, and how fast we'd find out.

In five years

Deeper, not sideways. I'd want to know identity well enough to argue about it, not just make it look better, and that takes a couple of years inside a domain.

And I'd want the design-and-code practice to be the team's rather than mine. Documented and shared, with patterns and coded components other designers reach for, so prototyping doesn't depend on one person being in the room.
