01 Where I am now

“Equally comfortable designing experiences and bringing them to life in code.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

02 Code as a design tool

“Use code as a design tool, not simply as an implementation medium.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

That sentence is unusually precise for a job ad, and it describes how I already work.

Vera is the clearest example. It checks supplement and wellness claims against scientific evidence: paste a social post, and it identifies the real product, grades each claim against peer-reviewed research, and shows live study counts pulled from OpenAlex. Behind it sits a Cloudflare Worker fetching posts server-side, a model doing identification, and real research data coming back live.

I led product design and engineering. It went from a rough "how might we" to something that runs, in front of people, in two days at the ZOE Women in Tech Hackathon.

  • 2 daysidea to working build
  • 3 inputslink, screenshot, brand name
  • Live datanothing mocked

A prototype in code exists to answer a question you can't answer in a design file. Here the question was whether a model could identify a real product reliably enough to build a product on top of it. The answer was yes, but only with a rule attached, which I'll come back to. Open Vera or read the case study.

03 Prototyping before implementation

“Rapidly prototype workflows and usability concepts before implementation.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Prototyping to explore is one thing. Prototyping to settle an argument is what I find useful.

At Omaze UK, the Monthly Millionaire subscription launched at around £650k a month against a £1m+ forecast. Funnel data showed mobile users engaging almost entirely with the bottom half of the screen, and a customer base skewing 45 and over. That told us where attention actually was, so we stopped debating layout and rebuilt around reachable actions, value and trust above the fold, and type sized for a real phone in real light.

Then I tested the things the data couldn't answer, one hypothesis at a time.

  • +7.3%trust signals above the fold
  • +4.8%winner reaction video hero
  • +8.5%subscription reframed as best value

The third is the one I argued hardest for and the one with real risk attached: the page had been built to lead with pay as you go, so promoting the subscription could simply have moved revenue from one pocket to another. We measured both. It didn't. 35% overall conversion uplift, £1.5m in a month, sustained above £1m. Read the case study.

04 Complex enterprise software

“Design user experiences for complex enterprise SaaS applications.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Paycada is the one I'd bring to this. An automated invoice chasing platform in regulated fintech, where I was the sole designer for three years, from the first idea through to a shipped product with a design system underneath it. Platform, marketing site and brand.

The interesting part isn't the workflow, it's what the workflow is for. Chasing an invoice means asking your customer for money without damaging the relationship you have with them. Automate it badly and you've built a machine that irritates the people who pay you. So a lot of the design work was about control and tone: what goes out automatically, what a human sees before it goes, how far an escalation is allowed to travel before someone has to decide. Regulated, financial, and used by people who live in it all day.

Veva is the second one, and it's about a different kind of complexity: one system serving two people with nothing in common. A mobile app controlling EV home charge points, used by the homeowner who lives with it for years and the engineer who has one visit to make it work.

I committed the design to the installer first, because that's the gate. If the charge point never gets on the network, the owner never sees a schedule, and their first experience of something they just paid for is a call to support. One decision followed from it: commissioning cannot depend on the home network being reachable, so the app detects the situation and falls back to a local hotspot from the charge point itself. The cleaner flow, the one assuming working wifi and correct credentials, is a better-looking design file and it fails in a driveway. Veva case study · Paycada case study.

05 Modern frontend

“Build prototypes using modern frontend technologies.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

You're reading the answer to this one.

This page is hand-built HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript, no framework and no build step. The contrast tool further down is about thirty lines of real JavaScript implementing the WCAG relative luminance formula: sRGB linearisation, the 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 coefficients, the 0.05 offsets. It isn't a picture of a tool. It runs.

For anything larger I work in React via Next.js, with TypeScript, Tailwind and Framer Motion, plus Python on the backend, Cloudflare Workers, Supabase, and Rive when something needs to move properly. Veva's eight charging states were built in Rive and handed to iOS and Android as JSON.

Honest note, because you list it: I haven't used Vue. I've picked up enough frameworks by now that I'm not worried about it, but I'd rather say so than let you find out in week two.

07 Accessibility and WCAG

“Solid understanding of accessibility standards and WCAG guidelines.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

On Paycada I ran a contrast audit across the component set and found two live failures that had shipped, because nobody had checked a hover state against its own background rather than against white.

Since then I check rather than assume. I measured my own portfolio the same way, computing relative luminance from the live CSS: the hero word "experiences" runs as a gradient, and its stops sit at 2.1:1, 3.0:1 and 3.7:1 against the near-white background. It's large display text, so it needs 3:1, and for part of every cycle it doesn't make it. Every accent on this page is your red at 5.4:1, which clears large display text and body text both.

Here's the tool, working, on the page. Every colour listed is one from my own system, or yours.

Contrast checker

Verify before you trust it. Body text at 16 pixels
2.07:1 Body text 4.5:1 Large text 3:1

Large text means 24px and above, or 18.66px and above when bold. Anything smaller has to reach 4.5:1.

This page passes its own test. Focus is visible throughout, motion respects prefers-reduced-motion, and the headings run in order.

08 Design systems and coded components

“Contribute to the design system, reusable patterns and coded component libraries.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

The Paycada system emerged in the work rather than being formalised upfront, and I've since gone back and articulated it properly as three tiers: brand primitives, semantic aliases, then component tokens. Full state coverage across the set, so nobody has to invent a disabled style at four o'clock on a Friday.

The thing I care about most now is drift. When I build with AI assistance the failure mode isn't broken code, it's plausible code: a spacing value quietly invented, a near-miss colour, a component rebuilt instead of reused. It's 90% right, which is more dangerous than 50%, because you don't see it until it has spread across a dozen files.

My current answer is to write the system's rules into a file the agent reads before it generates anything. It helps. It's still a patch on something that should be structural, and turning it into something a whole team can rely on is one of the problems I'd most like to work on properly.

09 AI-native tooling

“Leverage AI-native tools to accelerate ideation and generate realistic prototype data.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

Three things I'd say about this, all from doing it rather than reading about it.

Realistic data is the whole game

A prototype filled with placeholder text tests nothing. People respond to the content, not the layout, and lorem ipsum hides every problem you actually needed to find: the name that's too long, the empty state nobody designed, the number that breaks the column. Vera was built on live data for exactly that reason, and generating believable data is now the first thing I set up rather than the last.

Three versions beat one argument

Building fast means I can put three real directions in front of a team instead of defending one. That changes the conversation from "do you like it" to "which of these is wrong, and why", which is a much better meeting.

Speed is the smaller half of it

The real gain isn't hours saved, it's the number of questions you're willing to ask. When trying something costs twenty minutes rather than two days, you test the idea you'd otherwise have talked yourself out of.

10 AI-assisted experiences

“Design and prototype AI-assisted experiences.” Ping Identity, Senior UX Design Developer

This is the part of the role I want most, and I've had to make the decision that matters.

Vera's users are people being sold to by influencers they already trust, and we were asking them to move some of that trust to us. A generative model will always produce something. If it half recognised a product and filled in plausible ingredients, we'd be doing exactly what the influencer was doing, in a more authoritative voice.

If the system can't verify a field, that field comes back blank rather than invented.

A visible gap instead of a confident guess. It makes the product look less capable on some inputs, and that was the trade I wanted, because one fabricated ingredient list destroys the whole premise. Two things followed: we score the claim against the evidence rather than the brand, with study counts linked to source so anyone can check us, and we labelled the score honestly as a prototype heuristic rather than dressing it up as a scientific instrument.

Users don't need a system to always be right. They need to be able to tell when it isn't.

What I'd want to prototype here

You mention conversational interfaces, guided configuration and intelligent recommendations. Guided configuration is the one I'd go at first, because it's where that rule bites hardest. An admin configuring identity policy is making a decision with consequences, and a system that proposes a setting needs to show where the proposal came from, how confident it is, and what changes if it's wrong. Model proposes, human commits, evidence visible on the value itself, correction available in one interaction rather than buried in settings.

That's a pattern rather than a screen, which is why it belongs in a component library rather than in one flow.

11 Why you

Why Ping

Two reasons, and both are things I think I can solve for you rather than things I admire from a distance.

You've said the future isn't settled yet. Design and implementation are converging through modern tooling and AI-native workflows, and you want someone to help define what that looks like here. I've been living inside that convergence for two years and I've already hit the failure modes, drift being the big one. I'd be arriving with working opinions rather than theories, and I'd much rather develop them with a team than alone at my kitchen table.

And trust is about to become a design problem for you. Your products increasingly make an automated decision about whether someone is who they say they are. I've had to make that call on my own product: what should a system do when it isn't sure. A confident wrong answer gets accepted. A visible gap gets checked. That question will turn up in every AI-assisted feature you build, and I've had to answer it once already.

12 Straight answers

What I don't have

Identity, authentication and cybersecurity

None. It's the domain and I'd be learning it. What I do bring is regulated fintech and healthtech, so designing inside a constraint you cannot argue with is familiar. My instinct is that every security product lives on the same tension: more verification means more friction, more friction means fewer people complete, and there's no clever way to make that disappear. You only choose, step by step, which side you're paying. I'd want to find out quickly where Ping pays it.

Production engineering inside someone else's codebase

I build and ship my own products end to end, and Claude Code is part of that workflow daily. But I direct and review rather than write from a blank file, and I haven't worked inside a large existing team codebase. I read what I ship, I can debug it, and I can explain every decision in it. I wouldn't claim to be an engineer who'd own your production architecture, and I don't think this role is asking me to.

Primary qualitative research

Most of my research has been behavioural and quantitative, plus usability and prototype testing. I haven't personally led an end-to-end interview programme. It's the thing I'm most actively closing.

13 What happens next

Where this goes

In the first month

  • Learn the domain by watching real admins configure something, not by reading the docs.
  • Find the step in your flows where people stall, and build a prototype that tests one fix rather than arguing about three.
  • Audit the design system for drift, the gap between what's documented and what's actually in production.
  • Keep saying the quiet part: which of these decisions would tell us we were wrong, and how fast would we find out?

In five years

Deeper, not sideways. I'd want to know identity well enough to argue about it rather than just make it look better, and that takes a couple of years inside a domain.

And I'd want the design-and-code practice to be something the team has rather than something I do. Right now the way I work is mine, held together by my own habits. In five years I'd want it documented and shared, with patterns and coded components other designers reach for, so the whole team is prototyping at a level that doesn't depend on any one person being in the room.
